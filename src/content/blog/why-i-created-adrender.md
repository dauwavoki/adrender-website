I didn't set out to build a SaaS company. I set out to stop doing something the same annoying way for the hundredth time.

For ten years I designed app interfaces — product design, UX, the actual screens people use every day. When AI started taking off, I moved into designing specifically for AI companies, with a focus on minimalism — figuring out what an AI-powered product should actually show someone versus what it's merely capable of showing. That's not a background I picked up recently to chase a trend. It's been my specialty through the exact period AI tools have been exploding. Alongside all of that, for over a decade, I've also been writing code — not as my main job, just because I wanted to build things myself instead of always describing them to someone else. None of those things alone explains why AdRender exists. It was watching them collide, over and over, on the exact same task.

**The task that never got easier**

Making a real batch of ad creative — not one ad, a real batch, the kind you actually need to run a proper test — never got faster no matter how good I got at the individual tools. Find a reference in one place. Build the visual in another. Write the copy somewhere else. Resize everything by hand for every platform. Repeat for every variant. Every tool in that chain was fine on its own. I was fast at every single one of them. And it still took hours to produce what should've taken minutes.

The problem was never any one tool being bad. It was that nothing in the chain knew about anything else in the chain. Every handoff between them was where the time actually went, and no tool I ever tried — including every AI ad generator that launched over the past few years — fixed the handoffs. They just replaced one step in a still-broken chain.

**What finally made me build it instead of tolerating it**

I kept waiting for someone to build the thing that actually closed the gap — one flow, brand to finished ad, no stitching between five different pieces of software. Enough AI ad tools launched that I assumed eventually one of them would just do it properly. Most of what showed up either looked obviously AI-generated in a way that was becoming its own liability with customers, or solved one narrow piece — a photo tool here, a video tool there — while leaving the actual assembly problem untouched.

I'd spent years by that point specifically designing minimal, focused interfaces for AI-powered products — I wasn't guessing at what "AI product design" should look like, it was already the work I did. Combined with a decade of app design before that and real hands-on coding ability, I had a combination most people building AI ad tools don't have — usually it's engineers guessing at what marketers need, or marketers with no way to build what they're picturing. I had both halves, plus the specific AI-product design experience most of them were missing too. So I built it.

**What I actually wanted to exist**

Not a smarter prompt box. Not another template library bolted onto a generic AI wrapper. I wanted to type in a URL, or connect a Shopify store, and have a real batch of on-brand ads — static and video — come out the other end, generated from structures that were already proven to work, not invented by an AI guessing at what an ad should look like. I wanted the thing I'd been manually assembling for two decades to just... happen.

That's the whole premise of AdRender. Not a bigger feature list. Not a cleverer AI. Just the actual gap I kept hitting, closed.
