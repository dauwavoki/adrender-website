If you're running a Shopify store without a designer, Canva is probably the first tool you reached for — and for a lot of tasks, it's genuinely fine. The question worth asking honestly is whether it's still the right tool specifically for paid ad creative, where the bar is different than a social post or a flyer.

Canva's strength is flexibility: thousands of templates, drag-and-drop editing, and a low learning curve. That's also where the ceiling shows up for ads specifically. Templates aren't built from real, currently-running ad data — they're generic layouts that could be anything from a birthday invite to a webinar promo, retrofitted with your product photo. Nothing in Canva tells you whether the layout you picked resembles what's actually converting in your niche this week.

The other cost is time, and it compounds as your catalog grows. Every new product means starting from a template, manually dropping in your photo, rewriting the copy, resizing for whatever platform you're targeting, and repeating that for every variant. For a five-SKU store, that's manageable. For a real catalog — the kind most growing Shopify stores actually have — it turns into hours per week that don't get easier as you scale, only more repetitive.

There's also nothing in Canva that knows your brand beyond what you manually build into a brand kit and remember to apply every time. No product sync from your store, no auto-pull of your actual color palette and voice from your site, no memory of what worked last time you ran a similar product.

None of this means Canva is bad — it's a genuinely useful general design tool.
